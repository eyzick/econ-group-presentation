7

Time: 2 minutes. Focus on marginal incentives. Under a fixed hourly wage, the technician receives the same immediate pay for the third or fourth unit. PPP credits piece earnings for each unit, but actual pay stays at the guarantee until piece earnings exceed it. The rising segment represents the marginal incentive above the floor.

Ask whether the steeper line necessarily improves company profit. Students should mention quality, rework, customer satisfaction, accidents, and jobs that depend on costly travel.

Quick question (30 sec): When would one more installation add nothing to this week's paycheck? Ask for a prediction before tracing the flat and rising parts of the pay curve.

Likely answer: When piece earnings remain below the guarantee after the job. At the crossing, only the amount above the guarantee raises pay.

Optional follow-up: With a $440 guarantee, does moving piece earnings from $400 to $420 change pay? Debrief: No. Actual pay stays $440. Above the floor, the piece rate determines the marginal reward.

Beyond Safelite

Switching to piece rate carries the same tradeoffs in any job

Arguments for piece rate

  • Ties pay directly to output without monitoring effort
  • High-output workers self-select toward it
  • Removes the moral hazard built into a flat wage
  • Can raise total output substantially

Arguments against piece rate

  • Shifts income risk onto workers when output is noisy
  • Can distort effort toward measured tasks over quality or service
  • Adds measurement and administration cost
  • Risk-averse workers require a wage premium to accept it

The tradeoff is general: incentive strength and income insurance move in opposite directions whenever output depends on factors a worker cannot fully control.

Ask the room 20 sec

Name a job where piece rate would fit poorly. Why?

General labor economics reasoning, not specific to Safelite or the HBS case.

8

Time: 2 minutes. Step back from Safelite. Any move from hourly wage to piece rate makes the same basic trade: stronger effort incentives in exchange for pushing output risk onto the worker. This holds whether the job is windshield installation, sales, or piecework manufacturing.

Ask for a job where this fits poorly before naming one yourself. Team-based work, jobs with hard-to-measure quality, and jobs with highly variable external conditions (weather-dependent field work, for example) are weak fits, since output there reflects factors far outside individual control.

Quick question (20 sec): Name a job where piece rate would fit poorly. Why? Take one example and one reason.

Likely answer: Jobs with team output, safety-critical tasks, or output driven mostly by factors outside the worker's control.

Debrief: The same logic that makes piece rate attractive for Safelite technicians (observable, individually attributable output) is exactly what a poor-fit job lacks.

Transition: with the general tradeoff established, the next slides return to how Safelite specifically built its plan.

Time: 2 minutes. Clarify that PPP does not remove the floor. It changes the floor after a 12-week transition. The 30% cut creates the strongest controversy because it changes both incentives and income insurance.

Ask why Safelite included the 12-week period. Possible answers include learning, credibility, time to change habits, and a softer transition. Then ask why a 12-week transition may not solve winter seasonality.

Quick question (20 sec): Does a lower guarantee change the piece rate or the point where it starts to matter? Ask for a choice and a brief explanation.

Likely answer: It lowers the earnings threshold where piece pay determines actual pay. It does not change the rate per job.

Optional follow-up: Does this preserve the same amount of income insurance? Debrief: No. The floor falls even though the piece-rate slope stays the same.

Income insurance and motivation

Should PPP include a guaranteed minimum?

Why keep a minimum?

  • Protect income when offered work is low
  • May reduce discouragement and quitting
  • Let the firm pool shocks across markets

Where is the tradeoff?

  • Weaker pay incentives below the floor
  • Higher guaranteed labor cost
  • Piece rewards still apply above the floor

A floor can protect workers, but another job may add no take-home pay while piece earnings remain below it.

Ask the room 30 sec

Keep the minimum, lower it, or remove it? Why?

Economic interpretation of the guarantee and worker concerns: HBS case, pp. 6 and 8-9. Effects on motivation are hypotheses.

12

Time: 2 minutes. Explain that a minimum can make low-output weeks survivable, support learning, and reduce discouragement when workers cannot control offered volume. These are hypotheses, not measured morale effects in the case. Hourly pay also insures output risk conditional on paid hours and employment. Safelite can generally pool shocks more easily than one household.

The floor also creates a flat pay segment. A technician who cannot realistically reach the threshold may see little immediate monetary return to extra effort. Removing the floor sharpens the output-pay link but increases income risk and can discourage applicants or induce quits. Risk-averse workers may require higher expected pay to accept that risk.

Ask, 30 seconds: Keep the minimum, lower it, or remove it? Take a three-way vote and one reason. Debrief: income protection and marginal effort incentives can pull in different directions. Look for a credible chance to exceed the floor, coaching, and reliable job supply. Do not assume either full insurance or zero guarantee is always optimal.

Transition: seasonality makes the insurance question concrete. This question replaces the earlier risk-pooling poll and is included in the two minutes.

15

Time: 2 minutes. Separate treatment from selection. Some incumbents may install more because the marginal reward changes. Other workers may leave, while applicants who expect high output may join. Average productivity rises under either channel, but the managerial implications differ.

Recruitment: Higher potential earnings may attract fast applicants, while a low floor can deter skilled people who value stable income. Both effects are predictions. Assess applicant quality as well as application counts.

Screening: Safelite need not offer a menu of contracts for self-selection to occur. Workers choose between employment at Safelite and their outside options. Their choices can convey information, but acceptance alone does not separately identify productivity and risk tolerance.

Ask which departure worries Safelite most. Losing genuinely low-effort workers may help. Losing careful, experienced, risk-averse technicians may damage quality and training capacity.

Likely answer: Yes. A skilled worker may be risk-averse, face a weak route, or have a strong outside offer.

Optional follow-up: How would you separate selection from increased effort in the data? Debrief: Track incumbent output separately from the characteristics of workers who enter and leave.

Charlie Munger: “Reward and Punishment Superresponse Tendency”
Incentives can strongly shape behavior and judgment. (Paraphrase)

Goal: Fewer rats and less disease

Reward: Tails as evidence of dead rats

Result: Rat farming and live, tailless rats

Safelite: quantity and quality

Goal: Safe, reliable installations

Reward: Completed units and selected tasks

Risk: Rushed checks or neglected service

Discuss: What is Safelite’s “rat-tail” metric? What guardrail keeps it aligned with the goal?

Munger, The Psychology of Human Misjudgment (2005). Hanoi: Michael G. Vann interview (2020).
Safelite: HBS case, pp. 7 and 11. Quality risks are analytical possibilities, not documented misconduct.

16

Time: 3 minutes. Use 90 seconds for Munger and the historical analogy, 50 seconds for two responses, and 40 seconds for the debrief and transition. Credit Charlie Munger with the named tendency. The summary is a paraphrase, not a quotation. Strong responses to incentives need not be irrational.

In 1902, French colonial authorities in Hanoi offered a rat bounty, accepting tails as proof. Historian Michael G. Vann describes rat farming and the collection of tails while rats remained alive to breed. The reward made the proxy profitable even when the public-health goal suffered. This is an external analogy, not a story from the Safelite case or one attributed to Munger.

Ask: What is Safelite’s “rat-tail” metric? What guardrail keeps it aligned with the goal? Take two short responses. Likely answer: completed units can miss quality. Consider verified rework, quality thresholds, or audits. Require reliable attribution and preserve honest reporting. Do not imply Safelite technicians actually gamed the plan.

Debrief: Hanoi illustrates gaming a proxy. Safelite also faces multitasking: more attention to paid units can crowd out careful checks, cleanup, or cooperation even without fraud. Incentives cannot fix unavailable jobs or wrong parts by themselves. Transition: managers face their own competing incentives.

Keep a meaningful floor, adjust for verified firm-caused failures and low offered volume, add a small number of quality gates, and measure turnover and selection. The case makes a nationwide rollout with the full 30% cut hard to defend because Safelite still creates substantial output noise. Keeping hourly pay also leaves the moral-hazard problem unsolved.

Close with four lessons: incentives change effort; contracts allocate risk; measurement shapes behavior; and compensation works only when operations can support the promised output.
