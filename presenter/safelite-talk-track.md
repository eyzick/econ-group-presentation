# Safelite: presenter talk track and discussion script

Prepared September 27, 2026 for the consolidated 21-slide [HTML deck](../slides/safelite-performance-pay.html) and [PDF deck](../pdf/safelite-performance-pay.pdf). Aligned with presentation commit `e94e810`.

## How to use this script

The **Say**, **Ask**, **Debrief**, and **Transition** passages are suggested spoken language. Directions in square brackets and **Presenter cues** are private instructions. Adapt the wording to your voice. Do not read the anticipated answers before students respond.

The plan allocates **58 minutes, including student participation**, with two minutes for transitions or spillover. Time windows below are elapsed session time, not additional speaking time. They are targets to rehearse against, not a promise that reading at any speed fills the hour. Use pauses, point to the evidence, and let students explain their reasoning. Optional follow-ups replace spare time within a slide; they are not extra required questions.

Keep this script on a separate device, printed, or in a window the audience cannot see. The deck's **N** key displays notes over the same slide the audience sees. For the game, the presenter reveals answers by clicking an option and advances with the quiz's **Next** button. The game is local to the HTML file, with no automatic scoring or student-phone connection.

Prepare a timer and a place to tally votes. Students can signal A-D using one to four fingers. For the four-scenario exercise, assign four groups by seating area. No student devices are required. Any presenter handoffs should use the transition lines rather than repeat the previous section.

## Slide 1: Performance Pay at Safelite Auto Glass

**1 minute | 00:00-01:00**

**Say:**

“Imagine we are Safelite's management team at the decision point in this case. We have a national business, more customers to serve, and a productivity number that worries us: technicians average only 2.5 glass units per day.

“Management proposes paying technicians for what they install. But after twelve weeks, the plan also cuts their guaranteed hourly rate by about 30 percent. If output reflects effort, that could motivate people. If it reflects bad routing, wrong parts, or winter demand, it could punish people for problems they cannot solve.

“By the end, we'll vote on whether to adopt the plan, modify it, or keep hourly pay. As we work through the case, keep asking: what behavior does this contract reward, and who bears the risk?”

**Transition:** “Let's begin with your diagnosis, before we choose a solution.”

*Source: HBS case, pp. 5-6 and 9.*

## Slide 2: Why does an eight-hour day produce only 2.5 glass units?

**3 minutes | 01:00-04:00**

**Say:**

“Pick the explanation you would investigate first: low effort, scheduling, wrong parts, or demand and travel. Several can be true. For this first vote, choose the one you think management should test first.”

**Ask:** “Which do you choose, and what fact would change your answer?”

[Allow ten seconds of thinking. Take an A-D vote. Note the leading answer. Invite two or three students with different choices to give a brief explanation. Aim for about 90 seconds of responses.]

**Presenter cues:** Low-effort advocates may want comparisons among technicians on similar routes. Scheduling advocates may want job assignments and travel times. Parts advocates may want error and rescheduling records. Demand advocates may want offered jobs by season. Treat these as hypotheses to investigate, not established causal estimates.

**Debrief:**

“Notice that our explanations imply different interventions. If effort is the problem, pay incentives may help. If there aren't enough feasible appointments, a more motivated worker still cannot complete an unavailable job. If the warehouse supplies the wrong glass, we need to examine another person's work too.

“That distinction will matter when we decide whether lower output should mean lower pay.”

**If the room is quiet:** “Suppose two equally skilled technicians finish different numbers of jobs. What would you check before deciding one worked harder?”

**Transition:** “First, let's see what kind of organization these technicians work in.”

*Source: HBS case, pp. 4-5.*

## Slide 3: A national company inside a fragmented industry

**2 minutes | 04:00-06:00**

**Say:**

“In 1993, Safelite had about 500 stores and more than 3,000 employees, including roughly 1,000 installers and managers who installed. Its estimated market share was 12 percent, compared with about 6 percent for Harmon Glass. It was large relative to its rivals, but most of the market still lay elsewhere.

“Safelite had expanded through acquisitions. That created reach, but also duplicated capacity and incentives to focus on individual stores. At the same time, mobile service had grown to 44 percent of repairs and installations. Technicians increasingly brought service to the customer's location.

“That model offers convenience, but it makes routing and coordination central to productivity.”

**Ask, 20 seconds; optional if behind:** “Does a national chain make worker effort easier or harder to monitor?”

[Take hands for easier, harder, or both. Hear one explanation.]

**Debrief:** “Both is plausible. Scale makes personal supervision harder. A shared information system can make output easier to track. But output records still do not show every effort choice.”

**Optional follow-up:** “What important behavior would a completed-job count miss?”

**Transition:** “Safelite's response was to organize work across markets.”

*Source: HBS case, pp. 1-2. Market shares are case estimates.*

## Slide 4: The market system made output measurable

**2 minutes | 06:00-08:00**

**Say:**

“Follow a job through this process. A customer calls the central telephone unit. The system schedules an appointment and orders glass. Warehouse staff prepare the parts, and dispatch assigns a route. Only then does the technician travel to the customer and install the glass.

“Centralizing this work helps Safelite move capacity across stores instead of having one store turn away customers while another sits idle. The information system also lets management count completed units by technician.

“But look at how many inputs arrive before the technician gets to work. The company has improved measurement while also making individual performance depend on coordination.”

**Ask, 20 seconds:** “Where could one mistake stop a productive technician from finishing a job?”

[Take one answer and point to the relevant process step.]

**Debrief:** “A scheduling error, wrong part, or absent customer can all reduce completed output without proving low effort. We should ask who can prevent the failure, as well as who experiences it.”

**Optional follow-up:** “Who can prevent your example at the lowest cost?”

**Transition:** “That helps us interpret the 2.5-unit figure more carefully.”

*Source: HBS case, pp. 2-4.*

## Slide 5: 2.5 units account for only part of the workday

**2 minutes | 08:00-10:00**

**Say:**

“Management saw 2.5 units per day and knew a windshield installation generally took less than an hour. The natural reaction was to ask where the rest of the eight-hour day went.

“This bar illustrates that question. It is not a measured time study, and the case's glass-unit count includes more than windshields. We cannot conclude that the other 5.5 hours were all wasted.

“Travel, loading, checking parts, locating customers, and rescheduling can take real time. Some of those activities create value. Others may reflect preventable mistakes. Some lost time may also reflect low effort.”

**Ask:** “Which missing activity creates value, and which one signals avoidable slack?”

[Allow about 30 seconds. Take one example of each.]

**Debrief:** “Careful part checks can lower today's visible installation time while preventing a failed trip. Unnecessary waiting can reduce output without helping anyone. A useful metric should help distinguish those situations.”

**Transition:** “Economics gives us a framework for this measurement problem.”

*Source: HBS case, p. 5. The time allocation is illustrative.*

## Slide 6: The principal can observe output more easily than effort

**3 minutes | 10:00-13:00**

**Say:**

“Safelite is the principal. The technician is the agent. Safelite wants completed, safe, high-quality installations at a reasonable cost. The technician chooses pace, attention, part checking, and other actions management cannot observe continuously.

“Moral hazard describes the incentive problem that can arise when those actions are partly hidden. Under hourly pay, extra effort may not increase the current paycheck. There are still other motives, including pride, supervision, promotion, and keeping the job. The point is that the immediate monetary reward for another unit is weak.

“The expression on the left is a conceptual reminder, not an estimated equation. Output depends on effort, the operating system, demand, and luck. Rewarding output can encourage effort, but it also makes pay respond to those other inputs.”

**Ask, 30 seconds:** “If you can count installations, why can effort still be hard to observe?”

[Pause for five seconds. Take one answer.]

**Debrief:** “Two workers can exert the same effort and finish different numbers of jobs because their opportunities differ. Conversely, two workers can finish the same number with different effort if one has easier jobs. Output tells us something, but it doesn't isolate effort.”

**Say:** “That creates the design problem: we want a reward strong enough to change behavior, but we also need to consider the cost of making people bear income risk.”

**Optional follow-up:** “What extra information would make units per day a better signal?” [Listen for route length, task mix, offered jobs, parts errors, and rework.]

**Transition:** “Now let's see exactly how the pay formula changes the reward.”

*Source: economic interpretation of HBS case, pp. 5 and 8-9.*

## Slide 7: PPP rewards extra output above the guarantee

**2 minutes | 13:00-15:00**

**Say:**

“The horizontal line shows hourly earnings for a fixed number of hours. Completing another unit doesn't immediately change that pay.

“The PPP line has a flat section because of the guarantee. Piece earnings accumulate with completed jobs, but take-home pay stays at the floor until those earnings exceed it. Above that point, another paid installation increases actual pay. This drawing shows the initial floor equal to the former hourly earnings.”

**Ask, 30 seconds:** “When would one more installation add nothing to this week's paycheck?”

[Let someone identify the flat section before explaining the example.]

**Debrief:** “When piece earnings still do not exceed the guarantee. With a $440 guarantee, moving piece earnings from $400 to $420 leaves take-home pay at $440. Moving from $430 to $450 raises take-home pay by only $10. Once you're already above the floor, the full piece payment adds to earnings.”

**Presenter cue:** Those dollar examples illustrate the formula; they are not reported technician outcomes. Do not suggest PPP pays the hourly guarantee plus all piece earnings.

**Transition:** “The controversial part of the plan changes that flat section after twelve weeks.”

*Source: HBS case, pp. 6-7. Numerical threshold examples are illustrative.*

## Slide 8: A piece rate sits above a changing weekly guarantee

**2 minutes | 15:00-17:00**

**Say:**

“For the first twelve weeks, Safelite pays the higher of piece earnings or earnings at the technician's previous hourly rate. After twelve weeks, it lowers the guarantee rate by approximately 30 percent. The worker still receives whichever amount is higher.

“Think of the guarantee as income insurance. Lowering it reduces protection when output is low. It also lets piece earnings determine actual pay at a lower output threshold.”

**Ask, 20 seconds:** “Does a lower guarantee change the piece rate or the point where it starts to matter?”

**Debrief:** “It changes the threshold and the downside. It does not itself increase the payment per job or the slope above the threshold.”

**Say:** “The twelve-week transition gives workers time to learn the plan and adjust. But a transition period does not solve every problem. A technician who enters during a busy season could face a different earnings opportunity when winter arrives.”

**Optional follow-up:** “What would you want a worker to experience during those twelve weeks before judging the plan?”

**Transition:** “Safelite's worksheet makes the upside concrete. Let's examine its assumptions.”

*Source: HBS case, p. 6.*

## Slide 9: A five-unit day could produce $608.96 in a sample week

**3 minutes | 17:00-20:00**

**Say:**

“The worksheet assumes a daily mix of two curved tempered parts at $19 each and three windshields at $20 each. That's $38 plus $60, or $98 per day. Over five days, that produces $490.

“The sample then adds $112.51 for other installation work and $6.45 for items sold. Total performance pay is $608.96. Dividing by forty hours gives $15.22 per hour, rounded.

“Compared with the experienced wage range of $10 to $12 per hour, that looks attractive. But notice the workload. The glass-unit portion alone assumes five units per day, twice the reported 2.5-unit average, and the example also includes other tasks and sales.”

**Ask, 30 seconds:** “What must be true for the advertised $15.22 per hour to be realistic?”

[Take two brief conditions. Point back to the relevant worksheet assumptions.]

**Debrief:** “There must be enough available jobs, a feasible route, the right parts, and a task mix that supports those earnings. The work also has to fit the assumed forty hours. Rates varied by market, so this is a sample opportunity rather than a guaranteed earnings forecast for everyone.”

**Say:** “If I were evaluating the offer, I'd want to see more than this one successful week. I'd ask how often workers reach that level and what happens in a bad week. The distribution of earnings matters alongside the average.”

**Optional follow-up:** “Would the same expected pay be equally attractive if weekly income varied a lot?”

**Transition:** “Let's separate the factors workers can control from the factors the company supplies.”

*Source: HBS case, p. 7, Table 1. Wage comparison: p. 6.*

## Slide 10: Who controls each source of lost output?

**4 minutes | 20:00-24:00**

**Say:**

“Turn to a neighbor. These columns are a starting point, and you are allowed to disagree with them. Choose one factor that belongs in a different column or needs shared responsibility. Then decide whether it should reduce technician pay or trigger an operational fix. You have ninety seconds.”

[Use 30 seconds to explain, 90 seconds for pairs, 60 seconds for two reports, and 60 seconds for debrief and transition. Give a fifteen-second warning before discussion ends.]

**Ask when reporting back:** “Which factor did you choose, who can influence it, and how should pay respond?”

**Presenter cues:** Wrong parts are a good shared-responsibility example. The warehouse creates the error, but the technician can sometimes catch it before departure. Customer absence may be external, while appointment confirmation can reduce its likelihood. Accept more than one allocation if the proposed rule rewards useful prevention.

**Debrief:**

“The case reports wrong parts 10 to 20 percent of the time. That is a substantial operational problem. Paying for output may give technicians a reason to check parts more carefully. It does not make them responsible for creating every warehouse error.

“Our contract needs to recognize prevention without charging the technician for the full cost of a system failure. It also needs to encourage honest reporting. If reporting a problem always reduces pay, people may hide it.”

**Transition:** “This leads directly to who should bear the risk.”

*Source: HBS case, pp. 5 and 8-9.*

## Slide 11: High-powered incentives trade insurance for effort

**2 minutes | 24:00-26:00**

**Say:**

“A fixed hourly wage protects earnings when output varies, provided paid hours and employment remain unchanged. A piece rate makes earnings more responsive to output. That can improve effort, but it also transmits production shocks into the household budget.

“Risk aversion means a worker may prefer steadier pay to a riskier offer with the same expected value. To attract that worker, Safelite might need to offer higher expected earnings as compensation for the risk.”

**Ask, 20 seconds; optional if behind:** “Who can absorb a week of bad luck more easily: Safelite or one technician?”

**Debrief:** “Safelite generally has more capacity to pool shocks across workers and markets. One household has fewer ways to diversify its paycheck.”

**Say:** “That doesn't mean full insurance is always optimal. It can weaken the immediate reward for effort. In the standard incentive-insurance tradeoff, noisier output measures and more risk-averse workers tend to favor weaker output incentives or more protection.”

**Optional follow-up:** “Which risks could Safelite remove operationally instead of compensating workers to bear?”

**Transition:** “Seasonal demand makes this tradeoff especially visible.”

*Source: economic interpretation of HBS case, pp. 8-9.*

## Slide 12: Seasonal demand makes the guarantee economically important

**2 minutes | 26:00-28:00**

**Say:**

“Spring and summer were the busy seasons, when employers competed for technicians. Winter demand fell, and the industry often laid people off. The bars are schematic; the case provides no monthly volume series or exact seasonal ratios.

“Workers also had outside options. Managers described people changing employers for a dollar more per hour and the ability to take a company truck home. That tells us labor supply to Safelite could respond to small changes in the overall employment package.

“A worker's alternative matters just as much as Safelite's advertised upside.”

**Ask, 20 seconds:** “Should the guarantee change when winter brings too few jobs?”

**Debrief:** “A seasonal floor could protect workers from demand risk, but it costs money and requires clear rules. An alternative is to consider the number of feasible jobs offered, rather than judging low completed output in isolation.”

**Optional follow-up:** “What dispatch record would distinguish low demand from low effort?”

**Transition:** “Now let's put a dollar value on the proposed loss of protection.”

*Source: HBS case, p. 6.*

## Slide 13: The 30% guarantee cut is large in a household budget

**3 minutes | 28:00-31:00**

**Say:**

“Focus on the middle row. At $11 per hour, a forty-hour week provides a $440 floor. A 30 percent reduction makes the guarantee rate $7.70 and the weekly floor $308. That's $132 less guaranteed income for the same assumed hours.

“This is a reduction in the floor, not a prediction that every worker's actual paycheck will fall 30 percent. A technician with sufficiently high piece earnings can still earn more. But the downside is now real.

“The rows illustrate three wages from the case's experienced-worker range. Piece rates varied by market, and actual pay depends on the work available. The table doesn't give every technician the same earning opportunity.”

**Ask, 30 seconds:** “At $11 per hour today, would you accept PPP with a $308 weekly floor?”

[Take yes, no, and need-more-information votes. Hear one reason. If time allows, hear a contrasting reason.]

**Debrief:** “The answer depends on expected earnings, variability, family commitments, outside offers, and trust in dispatch. Management sees a reason to become more productive. A worker can see a secure paycheck shrinking before knowing whether the system will provide enough jobs.”

**Say:** “That is why communication matters, but communication cannot substitute for a credible earnings opportunity. A clearer brochure will not fix an unreliable route or missing glass.”

**Optional follow-up:** “Which single piece of information would most change your vote?”

**Transition:** “Different answers also predict who will stay and who will leave.”

*Source: HBS case, pp. 6 and 9; regional piece rates: p. 7, Table 1 note a. Calculations assume forty paid hours.*

## Slide 14: PPP changes who works at Safelite, not only how they work

**2 minutes | 31:00-33:00**

**Say:**

“There are two channels to distinguish. The effort effect occurs when the same technician changes behavior under the new pay plan. Selection occurs when the workforce changes because different people join, stay, or leave.

“Fast technicians who expect high piece earnings may find Safelite more attractive. Some lower-output workers may leave. But the plan can also lose people for reasons other than skill.”

**Ask, 20 seconds:** “Could PPP drive away a highly skilled technician?”

**Debrief:** “Yes. That person may value predictable income, face an unfavorable route, or receive a strong outside offer. A departure does not prove low productivity.”

**Say:** “This is a screening effect through employment choices. Safelite does not need to offer a menu of contracts for self-selection to occur. However, acceptance alone cannot tell us separately how skilled or risk-averse someone is.

“If average output rises, we need to compare incumbent performance separately from entry and exit before attributing the entire increase to effort.”

**Optional follow-up:** “What information about departing workers would help assess whether turnover is costly?”

**Transition:** “Even among workers who stay, the pay formula can change which tasks get attention.”

*Source: HBS case, pp. 6 and 8-9. Effort and selection channels are economic predictions, not reported post-rollout findings in this case.*

## Slide 15: Workers shift attention toward what the pay formula measures

**3 minutes | 33:00-36:00**

**Say:**

“The pay plan rewards completed units, certain other tasks, and sales. Quality, safety, customer explanations, and cooperation are harder to measure. When time is limited, workers may shift attention toward the activities with the clearest reward.

“Imagine a technician is already above the guarantee and can finish one more job by rushing a check or cleanup. The extra unit adds pay immediately. The cost of a quality failure may arrive later and may fall on someone else.”

**Ask:** “The next job adds pay but requires rushing. What guardrail changes the choice?”

[Allow about 60 seconds for two suggestions and a brief challenge to one.]

**Presenter cues:** Look for quality thresholds, verified rework measures, audits, and customer feedback. Ask whether a proposed penalty depends on an outcome the technician actually controlled. Do not present any penalty as an existing case provision.

**Debrief:**

“A credible quality measure can bring some of that delayed cost back into the decision. But a poorly designed penalty can encourage workers to hide problems or avoid difficult jobs. We need reliable attribution and a reason to report defects honestly.

“This is the multitasking problem: stronger incentives on one measurable task can reduce effort on another valuable task. More completed units create value only if the work meets the required standard.”

**Transition:** “Managers face their own version of this tradeoff.”

*Source: pay components and quality goals in HBS case, pp. 7 and 11. Rushing scenario and guardrails are analytical examples.*

## Slide 16: Manager incentives depend on store size and job design

**2 minutes | 36:00-38:00**

**Say:**

“At a large operation, the manager's job is to coordinate people. In the case's smaller stores, managers spend much more time installing themselves. The table ranges from no installation time in the largest categories to full installation time in the smallest.

“Rewarding personal installations can encourage lean staffing. It can also make another technician feel like competition for jobs the manager could complete. That may discourage hiring or reduce time spent coaching.”

**Ask, 20 seconds; optional if behind:** “Should a store manager's bonus depend on personal output or the team's output?”

[Take a vote for personal, team, or a mix. Hear one rationale.]

**Debrief:** “Team output can reward coordination, but it makes individual contribution harder to identify and can create free-riding. Personal output is easier to count, but it can pull attention away from management. A sensible measure depends on the job.”

**Optional follow-up:** “Would you use the same mix in a small store and a large dispatch center?”

**Transition:** “Let's try designing rules for the specific problems we've identified.”

*Source: HBS case, pp. 7-8, Table 2.*

## Slide 17: Design a pay rule for four shocks

**4 minutes | 38:00-42:00**

**Say:**

“Each group gets one scenario. Choose full piece credit, partial credit, guarantee only, or a quality penalty. You have ninety seconds to choose a rule and explain what behavior it encourages. There is no single answer key, but your rule must address who controlled the outcome.”

[Spend 30 seconds assigning groups, 90 seconds on discussion, 80 seconds on four twenty-second reports, and 40 seconds on synthesis.]

**Ask each group:** “What's your rule, and why would it improve behavior?”

**Presenter cues for the reports:**

- **Wrong glass caught early:** Credit prevention or verified lost time. Full completed-job credit might overpay for unfinished work or invite false claims. Reward the check without removing warehouse accountability.
- **Customer absent:** Partial credit or protection can recognize a verified arrival. Consider whether contact and confirmation procedures were followed. The rule should not encourage needless trips.
- **Winter demand:** A floor tied to documented offered volume can insure demand risk. Ask what happens when a worker refuses a feasible job.
- **Repeat repair:** Investigate the cause before imposing a penalty. Worker error, defective parts, or another technician's installation require different treatment. Keep an incentive to report and correct the problem.

**Debrief:** “Every rule balances motivation, insurance, and verification. Protecting people from things outside their control is useful only if the system can distinguish those events reliably. And a rule that rewards hiding problems can make the operation worse.”

**Transition:** “Let's check the economic concepts before making the final decision.”

*Source: scenarios informed by HBS case, pp. 5 and 8-9. Proposed rules are discussion options.*

## Slide 18: Five questions on incentive economics

**5 minutes | 42:00-47:00**

**Say:** “Stay with your teams. We have five questions. Choose A, B, C, or D. Give yourselves one point for the right answer and another if your team can explain why. Keep your own score; ten points are possible.”

[Allow about twenty seconds for setup, then roughly fifty seconds per question, leaving thirty seconds for the closing tally. Ask teams to discuss briefly, vote together, then reveal. Invite one team to explain each answer while the others compare their reasoning.]

### Question 1

**Read:** “Under hourly pay, what happens to immediate pay after one extra installation?”

A. It rises by the piece rate. B. It changes little or not at all. C. It falls because travel rises. D. It depends only on quality.

**Answer: B.** “For the same paid hours, another completed unit does not directly raise hourly earnings. Other incentives can still matter.”

### Question 2

**Read:** “A wrong part supplied on 10%-20% of jobs creates what contract problem?”

A. Perfect measurement. B. A pure effort signal. C. Noise in measured performance. D. No economic consequence.

**Answer: C.** “Completed output reflects warehouse performance as well as technician effort. The technician may still help by checking parts.”

### Question 3

**Read:** “Who bears more winter demand risk after the guarantee falls 30%?”

A. The insurer. B. The customer. C. Safelite alone. D. The technician.

**Answer: D.** “A lower floor exposes more of the technician's income to low job volume. Safelite still bears business risk too.”

### Question 4

**Read:** “If low-output workers leave after PPP, what raises average productivity?”

A. Selection. B. Moral hazard. C. Insurance. D. Seasonality.

**Answer: A.** “The workforce composition changes. Average output can rise even if each remaining worker's behavior stays the same.”

### Question 5

**Read:** “Paying for quantity while quality stays hard to measure creates what concern?”

A. Risk pooling. B. Multitasking distortion. C. Perfect alignment. D. Lower marginal incentives.

**Answer: B.** “Attention may shift toward measured output and away from quality or service.”

**Close:** “Who has eight points or more? Which explanation was hardest? Keep that distinction in mind as we compare the contracts.”

**Presenter cues:** Correct-answer sequence is **B, C, D, A, B**, matching the HTML. Clicking any answer reveals the correct choice. Use the quiz's own Next button for the next question. With the PDF, read the alternatives from this script and use hands or fingers for votes. Before resuming slide navigation, click a non-button area so keyboard focus is outside the quiz controls.

*Source: HBS case, pp. 5-9, interpreted through the economic concepts discussed above.*

## Slide 19: Three contracts solve different problems

**2 minutes | 47:00-49:00**

**Say:**

“These ratings are judgments we can challenge, not results from an experiment. Hourly pay gives income protection for fixed hours but weak direct rewards for extra units. PPP strengthens output incentives above the floor while exposing workers to more risk. Modified PPP tries to balance the two, at the cost of complexity.

“The case also reports complaints about dispatch managers' effort and equal job allocation. That suggests a possible incentive problem upstream. Equal allocation itself is not proof of shirking; it can also reflect fairness or other constraints. The practical point is that technician pay alone cannot solve every dispatch problem.”

**Ask, 20 seconds; optional if behind:** “Which rating in this table would you challenge?”

**Debrief:** “Turnover is a good example. Hourly pay does not eliminate seasonal layoffs or competing offers, and PPP could retain some high performers. We should treat the ratings as testable expectations.”

**Optional follow-up:** “What would you measure to resolve your disagreement?”

**Transition:** “Here is what a modified contract might include.”

*Source: HBS case, pp. 5-9. Ratings are analytical judgments.*

## Slide 20: Modified PPP can preserve the slope and improve the floor

**2 minutes | 49:00-51:00**

**Say:**

“One possible redesign keeps piece rates while reconsidering the guarantee. Safelite could reduce the floor more gradually or protect workers when documented offered volume is low. Keeping the piece rate preserves the slope above the floor, although a higher floor changes how often workers reach that segment.

“Credits could recognize verified customer absences or warehouse failures when the technician followed procedure. Quality measures could discourage rushing, provided failures are attributable and workers still have a reason to report them.

“Mobile and in-store work may also need different baselines because travel changes the opportunity to complete jobs. The rules need enough precision to help without becoming impossible to administer.

“Before expanding, I'd test output, labor cost per unit, quality, earnings variability, and departures across comparable markets and seasons. I'd track the same workers over time and separately examine hiring and exit. Otherwise we could mistake a workforce change or an operating improvement for an effort response.

“These are proposed design choices. The case does not report that Safelite adopted them or show us the outcome of this decision.”

**Transition:** “Now you have to choose which tradeoff you are willing to accept.”

*Source: proposals based on HBS case, pp. 2 and 5-9.*

## Slide 21: What should Safelite do now?

**7 minutes | 51:00-58:00**

### Frame and initial vote: 45 seconds

**Say:**

“Choose A, B, or C. A implements the existing plan and its guarantee reduction. B retains performance pay but adds protections and measurement. C keeps hourly pay while addressing possible operating bottlenecks.

“Each has a cost. A creates income and turnover risk. B costs more to design and administer. C leaves weaker immediate pay incentives. Vote now.”

[Record the vote. Keep the presenter's recommendation private until after the debate.]

### Prepare arguments: 2 minutes

**Say:** “Talk with someone who chose the same option, or defend your choice to a neighbor. Prepare your strongest economic reason and one piece of evidence that could reverse your choice.”

[Give a thirty-second warning. Invite one spokesperson for each option. If an option has no supporters, briefly state its strongest argument yourself.]

### Hear the alternatives: 90 seconds

**Ask:** “What is your strongest reason, and what would change your mind?”

[Allow thirty seconds per option. A useful argument for A identifies substantial controllable slack and feasible available jobs. B should explain which protection justifies its complexity. C should explain why operating constraints deserve priority and how it would address effort meanwhile.]

### Challenge and revote: 75 seconds

**Ask:** “Which argument changed your assessment of the risk?”

[Take two brief responses, then revote. Ask one person who switched to explain why. Do not treat a vote for B as the only correct outcome.]

### Suggested synthesis and close: 90 seconds

**Say, if this matches your recommendation:**

“My recommendation is a controlled pilot of modified PPP. The case gives us a reason to improve effort incentives, but it also shows substantial output constraints outside a technician's control. I'd keep a meaningful floor, test limited protections for verified system failures, and measure quality and retention alongside output.

“I would change that recommendation if the evidence showed that the existing system reliably supplied enough feasible work, quality stayed strong, and the lower guarantee did not drive away valuable technicians. “The main lesson is that compensation shapes both behavior and the workforce. A performance measure rewards some activities more clearly than others. A guarantee allocates risk. And the operating system determines whether employees can actually achieve the output the pay plan promises.

“When evaluating performance pay, we should ask what people can control, what valuable work the measure misses, and what evidence would show that the contract improves total performance.”

## Backup responses for likely questions

These are optional preparation notes, not additional material to deliver on top of the 58-minute plan.

**“What actually happened to productivity?”** The supplied A case ends with the decision and does not report a measured post-rollout productivity effect. This presentation evaluates the choice using the information in that case. Later empirical results would need separate sourcing.

**“Does this mean hourly workers are lazy?”** No. Hourly pay has a weaker immediate monetary link between another unit and pay, but supervision, professional standards, reputation, and future employment can all motivate effort. The case also identifies operational constraints.

**“Why not eliminate the guarantee entirely?”** That would expose workers to more output risk and remove the flat guarantee segment, but it could require higher expected compensation, worsen retention, and increase quality pressure. It is a different contract choice, not a free improvement.

**“Why reward a worker for a job they didn't complete?”** A limited credit can insure a verified system failure or reward a useful preventive action. It should be designed against false claims and should not automatically equal full completed-job pay.

**“Is the 30% cut a 30% cut in actual earnings?”** It is approximately a 30% cut in the guarantee rate after twelve weeks. Actual pay is the higher of piece earnings and the guarantee. Hours and offered work also matter.

**“Isn't giving more work to fast technicians unfair?”** It can improve short-run throughput but may reduce other workers' learning opportunities or earnings, and it can make observed productivity partly reflect assignment. Evaluate feasible routing, quality, fairness, and incentives together. Equal allocation alone does not prove low managerial effort.

**“How would you test PPP?”** If feasible, assign comparable markets to contract variants and observe them over relevant seasons. Track output, labor and rework costs, quality, worker earnings, hours, offered jobs, quits, and hiring. Analyze incumbent behavior and workforce composition separately. A simple before-and-after average can confound the pay change with demand or operating changes.

## Facilitation recovery lines

**Silence:** “Take five seconds. Start with one example rather than a complete solution.”

**A long answer:** “Let me capture your main point as [brief paraphrase]. I'd like one contrasting view before we move on.”

**An answer confuses guarantee with bonus:** “Let's return to the formula. Safelite pays the higher amount, not the guarantee plus all piece earnings.”

**A claim goes beyond the case:** “That's a reasonable hypothesis. What observation would we need to verify it?”

**Running behind:** “I'll leave that follow-up for the final discussion so we have time to make the decision.”

**Finishing early:** “Choose a different option from your own. What is its strongest economic argument, and what evidence would make it the better choice?”
