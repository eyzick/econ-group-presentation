# Safelite — high-level presenter reference

**Core question:** Should Safelite roll out performance pay (PPP) as designed, modify it, or retain hourly pay?

**Main takeaway:** Output is not effort alone. A good contract balances motivation, income protection, quality, and the opportunity to perform.

## Numbers to remember

| Case fact or calculation | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| **2.5 glass units/day** average | Low output motivates the proposal; it does not prove shirking. (p. 5) |
| **10–20%** wrong-parts frequency | Upstream mistakes make output a noisy effort measure. (p. 5) |
| **44%** mobile service | Travel and dispatch affect feasible output. (p. 2) |
| **12 weeks**, then about **30%** lower guarantee rate | The plan shifts more downside risk to workers. (p. 6) |
| **$11/hour → $7.70/hour** guarantee | At 40 hours: **$440 → $308**, or **$132 less guaranteed**; illustrative calculation. |
| **$608.96/week ≈ $15.22/hour** sample | Assumes five glass units/day plus other work and sales in 40 hours—not a typical-pay forecast. (p. 7, Table 1) |

**Pay rule:** Actual pay is the **higher of piece earnings or the guarantee**, not both added together. Lowering the floor changes the threshold where piece pay matters; it does not change the piece rate.

## Economics in plain language

- **Principal–agent / moral hazard:** Safelite cannot fully observe technicians’ effort. Hourly pay weakens the immediate monetary reward for another unit, but other motives remain.
- **Measurement and control:** Completed jobs reflect effort **plus** routing, parts, customer availability, demand, and luck.
- **Guarantee and morale:** A floor may reduce discouragement and quitting, but extra output below it may add no pay. A credible path above the floor matters.
- **Risk shifting:** Piece pay exposes workers to output fluctuations. Seasonal demand makes the guarantee valuable.
- **Effort versus selection:** The same people may work differently; different people may join, stay, or leave. Separate these effects when evaluating productivity.
- **Multitasking:** Rewarding quantity can crowd out safety, quality, cooperation, or customer service.
- **Munger connection:** Charlie Munger’s “Reward and Punishment Superresponse Tendency” highlights incentives’ power over behavior and judgment. Hanoi’s 1902 rat-tail bounty encouraged rat farming. Ask whether the rewarded proxy serves the goal. This is an analogy, not evidence of Safelite misconduct.
- **Labor supply and turnover:** Outside offers and preferences for stable income matter. A skilled worker can reject PPP.
- **Fairness:** Judge contribution and opportunity, not group stereotypes. Investigate assignments, training, hours, and quality before interpreting demographic pay gaps. Hourly pay and salary can also embed unequal opportunities.
- **Manager incentives:** Personal installation rewards may compete with hiring, coaching, and team coordination.

## Final decision

**A — PPP as designed:** Stronger incentives above the floor; greater income and turnover risk.

**B — Modify PPP:** Retain piece rates; reconsider the floor, verified system-failure protections, and quality measures. Tradeoff: cost and complexity.

**C — Keep hourly pay:** Preserve income protection and address bottlenecks. Tradeoff: weaker immediate output incentives.

**Suggested position, not a case finding:** Pilot B; track output, cost per unit, quality, earnings variability, and retention across seasons. Separate incumbent changes from hiring and exit.

**Watch-outs:** The 30% cut concerns the guarantee—not every paycheck. The A case does not establish a post-rollout productivity effect. Protect the final debate if running late; skip optional questions on slides 3, 16, and 19. Target: **58 minutes + 2-minute buffer**.
