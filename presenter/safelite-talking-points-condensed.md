**Slide 1 — Performance Pay at Safelite Auto Glass** (1 min)
- National business, techs average only 2.5 glass units/day
- New plan: pay per install, but cuts guaranteed hourly rate ~30% after 12 weeks
- Effort-driven output → incentive works; routing/parts/weather-driven output → punishes for things outside their control
- End-of-class vote: adopt, modify, or keep hourly
- Running question: what behavior does this contract reward, and who bears the risk?
- Transition: diagnosis before solution
- Source: HBS case pp. 5–6, 9

**Slide 2 — Why only 2.5 units/day?** (3 min)
- A–D vote: low effort / scheduling / wrong parts / demand & travel — which to test first
- 10 sec think → vote → 2–3 students explain (~90 sec)
- Cues: effort → compare similar-route techs; scheduling → job assignments/travel; parts → error records; demand → offered jobs by season
- Debrief: different causes → different fixes (pay incentives help effort, not unavailable jobs or bad parts)
- If quiet: ask what you'd check before saying one tech worked harder than another
- Transition: look at the org these techs work in
- Source: HBS case pp. 4–5

**Slide 3 — National company, fragmented industry** (1 min)
- ~500 stores, 3,000+ employees, 12% share vs. Harmon's 6%
- ~1,000 installers; 44% of work is mobile → routing/coordination matter
- Ask (10 sec, optional): does scale make effort easier or harder to monitor?
- Debrief: both — less personal supervision, more shared data
- Transition: follow a job through the system
- Source: HBS case pp. 1–2 (market shares are case estimates)

**Slide 4 — Market system made output measurable** (1 min)
- 4 steps: call → scheduling/ordering → warehouse/dispatch → travel/install
- Centralizing tracks output by tech, but each tech depends on others' work
- Ask (10 sec): where could one mistake stop a productive tech?
- Debrief: wrong parts/bad schedule can lower output without proving low effort
- Transition: matters for interpreting the 2.5-unit number
- Source: HBS case pp. 2–4

**Slide 5 — 2.5 units is only part of the day** (2 min)
- Install itself <1 hr — where did the other ~5.5 hrs go?
- Chart is illustrative, not a time study; unit count includes more than windshields
- Travel, loading, parts checks, locating customers, rescheduling all take real time — some add value, some don't
- Ask (~30 sec): name one valuable activity vs. one avoidable-slack activity
- Debrief: careful part checks look like "lost time" but prevent failed trips; unnecessary waiting helps no one
- Transition: economics gives us a framework for this
- Source: HBS case p. 5 (time allocation illustrative)

**Slide 6 — Output easier to observe than effort** (3 min)
- Safelite = principal, technician = agent; moral hazard risk when actions are hidden
- Hourly pay: extra effort doesn't change this week's paycheck (other motives still exist)
- Output = effort + system + demand + luck (conceptual, not an equation)
- Ask (30 sec, 5-sec pause): if installs are countable, why is effort still hard to observe?
- Debrief: same effort → different output (different opportunities); same output can hide different effort
- Tradeoff: reward strength vs. cost of pushing income risk onto workers
- Optional: what extra info would make units/day a better signal? (route length, task mix, offered jobs, parts errors, rework)
- Transition: see how the pay formula changes the reward
- Source: economic interpretation, HBS case pp. 5, 8–9

**Slide 7 — PPP rewards output above the guarantee** (2 min)
- Hourly: flat line, another unit doesn't change pay
- PPP: flat until piece earnings exceed guarantee, then rises
- Ask (30 sec): when does one more install add nothing to pay?
- Debrief: while piece earnings stay under the guarantee. E.g. $440 floor: $400→$420 piece earnings = no change; $430→$450 = only +$10
- Cue: dollar examples are illustrative, not reported outcomes
- Transition: the controversial part changes the flat section after 12 weeks
- Source: HBS case pp. 6–7 (thresholds illustrative)

**Slide 8 — Piece-rate tradeoffs apply anywhere** (2 min)
- Stronger $ incentive for output; can attract people expecting to earn more
- Doesn't fix measurement problems: unmeasured quality/cooperation get less attention; workers needing stable income need higher expected pay
- Ask (20 sec): name a job where piece rate fits poorly, and why (team production, safety-critical, variable conditions)
- Transition: look at Safelite's actual plan
- Source: general economic reasoning

**Slide 9 — Piece rate above a changing guarantee** (2 min)
- First 12 weeks: higher of piece earnings or old hourly rate
- After 12 weeks: guarantee cut ~30%; still paid the higher of the two
- Guarantee = income insurance; lowering it cuts protection and lowers the threshold where piece pay takes over
- Ask (20 sec): does a lower guarantee change the rate, or just where it starts to matter?
- Debrief: changes threshold/downside only, not the per-job rate
- 12-week transition helps learning/adjustment, but doesn't fix someone who starts busy and hits winter
- Optional: what should a worker experience in those 12 weeks before judging the plan?
- Transition: Safelite's worksheet makes the upside concrete
- Source: HBS case p. 6

**Slide 10 — $608.96 sample week** (2 min)
- 2 tempered + 3 windshields/day = $98/day, $490/wk; +$112.51 other work +$6.45 sales = $608.96 = $15.22/hr
- Exceeds $10–12 experienced range, but assumes 5 units/day (2x reported average)
- Ask (30 sec): what must be true for $15.22/hr to be realistic?
- Debrief: enough feasible jobs, correct parts, mix fits in 40 hrs; rates vary by market — illustration, not a typical-pay forecast
- Transition: separate worker control from what the company supplies
- Source: HBS case p. 7 Table 1; wage comparison p. 6

**Slide 11 — Who controls lost output?** (4 min)
- 30 sec intro columns, 2 min discussion, 90 sec summary/transition
- Ask: are these columns right? Which factors should affect pay vs. need an operational fix?
- Follow-up: who can influence each factor, and how should pay respond?
- Cues: wrong parts = shared responsibility (warehouse causes it, tech can sometimes catch it); no-show customer = external, but confirmations help
- Debrief: wrong parts occur 10–20% of the time — real operational problem. Pay-for-output can encourage checking, not blame for every warehouse error. Contract needs to reward prevention without charging the worker for a system failure, and preserve honest reporting
- Transition: leads to who bears the risk
- Source: HBS case pp. 5, 8–9

**Slide 12 — Should PPP include a minimum?** (2 min)
- Floor: makes bad weeks survivable, eases discouragement during learning/low job supply; company pools risk better than one household (hypotheses, not measured)
- But: below the floor, another job adds $0 to pay; removing the floor sharpens the link but raises risk and may deter applicants/cause quits
- Ask (30 sec): keep, lower, or remove the minimum? (3-way vote + reason)
- Debrief: protection vs. motivation pull different directions — look at the credible chance to beat the floor, reliable dispatch, coaching; neither extreme is automatically best
- Transition: seasonal demand makes this concrete
- Source: economic interpretation, HBS case pp. 6, 8–9

**Slide 13 — Seasonal demand & the guarantee** (2 min)
- Spring/summer = busy, competition for techs; winter demand drops, often layoffs (bars schematic, no exact case data)
- Workers have outside options — e.g. switching for $1/hr more + a company truck
- Ask (20 sec): should the guarantee change when winter brings too few jobs?
- Debrief: seasonal floor protects against demand risk but costs money/needs clear rules; alternative — judge by jobs offered, not completed output alone
- Optional: what dispatch record would separate low demand from low effort?
- Transition: put a dollar value on the proposed loss of protection
- Source: HBS case p. 6

**Slide 14 — 30% cut is large for a household** (3 min)
- At $11/hr: 40-hr floor $440 → cut to $308 (–$132/wk guaranteed)
- Floor reduction, not a guaranteed 30% pay cut — high piece earners can still earn more, but the downside is real
- Table shows 3 wage tiers from the case; rates vary by market, not every tech has the same opportunity
- Ask (30 sec): at $11/hr today, would you accept PPP with a $308 floor? (yes/no/need info + reason)
- Debrief: depends on expected earnings, variability, family needs, outside offers, trust in dispatch
- Communication can't substitute for a credible earnings opportunity
- Optional: what single fact would most change your vote?
- Transition: different answers predict who stays vs. leaves
- Source: HBS case pp. 6, 9; regional rates p. 7 Table 1 note a; assumes 40 paid hrs

**Slide 15 — PPP changes who works there, not just how** (2 min)
- Effort effect: same tech changes behavior. Selection effect: workforce composition changes (who joins/stays/leaves)
- Fast techs may be drawn in by high piece pay; low floor may deter skilled applicants needing stable income
- Ask (20 sec): could PPP drive away a highly skilled tech?
- Debrief: yes — may value predictable income, have a bad route, or a strong outside offer; departure ≠ low productivity
- Screening via employment choice — no contract menu needed for self-selection, but acceptance alone doesn't separate skill from risk tolerance
- If output rises, separate incumbent performance from entry/exit before crediting effort alone
- Optional: what info about departing workers tells us if turnover is costly?
- Transition: pay formula also changes which tasks get attention
- Source: HBS case pp. 6, 8–9 (effort/selection are predictions, not reported findings)
