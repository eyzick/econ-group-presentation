# Safelite Presenter Notes: Condensed, Flow Version

Each slide runs in presenting order: setup, ask, debrief, optional follow-up, then the line that hands off to the next slide.

**Slide 1: Performance Pay at Safelite Auto Glass** (1 min)
- Open: we're Safelite's management at the decision point. National business, but techs average only 2.5 glass units a day
- The proposal: pay per install, then cut the guaranteed hourly rate about 30% after 12 weeks
- The tension: if output reflects effort, that motivates. If it reflects routing, parts, or weather, it punishes people for problems they can't fix
- Tell them we vote at the end: adopt, modify, or keep hourly
- Frame all of it with one question: what behavior does this contract reward, and who bears the risk?
- Handoff: "Before we pick a solution, let's diagnose the problem."
- Source: HBS case pp. 5-6, 9

**Slide 2: Why only 2.5 units a day?** (3 min)
- Ask: which explanation would you test first: low effort, scheduling, wrong parts, or demand and travel? Several can be true, so pick the first one management should investigate
- Run it: 10 sec to think, A-D vote, then 2-3 students with different answers explain (~90 sec). If quiet: "What would you check before saying one tech worked harder than another?"
- Listen for: effort → compare techs on similar routes; scheduling → job assignments and travel times; parts → error and reschedule records; demand → offered jobs by season
- Debrief: each explanation points to a different fix. Low effort calls for incentives, bad scheduling or wrong parts call for operational fixes, and thin demand means no pay plan helps. Pay-for-output only works on the slice of the gap the technician controls, so we diagnose before choosing a contract
- Handoff: "To judge these explanations, we first need to see what kind of organization these techs work in."
- Source: HBS case pp. 4-5

**Slide 3: A national company in a fragmented industry** (1 min)
- Scale: ~500 stores, 3,000+ employees, 12% market share vs. Harmon's 6% (case estimates)
- ~1,000 installers, and 44% of work is mobile, which makes routing and coordination central to productivity
- Ask (10 sec, optional): does scale make effort easier or harder to monitor? Take one phrase
- Debrief: both. Scale means less face-to-face supervision, but central systems log every job by technician. So Safelite can measure output far better than it can observe effort, and that gap is what the whole case turns on
- Handoff: "Let's follow one job through that system."
- Source: HBS case pp. 1-2

**Slide 4: The market system made output measurable** (1 min)
- Walk the four steps: customer call → scheduling and glass ordering → warehouse and dispatch → travel and install
- Centralizing lets Safelite move capacity across stores and count output by tech, but each tech now depends on other people's work
- Ask (10 sec): where could one mistake stop a productive tech from finishing a job? Point to the step
- Debrief: the output number belongs to the system as much as the technician. A wrong part or a bad schedule shows up as low units even at full effort, so a raw count can blame the person for the system. That's the core measurement problem for any pay tied to units
- Handoff: "Keep that in mind, because it changes how we read the 2.5-unit number."
- Source: HBS case pp. 2-4

**Slide 5: 2.5 units is only part of the workday** (2 min)
- Management saw 2.5 units, knew an install takes under an hour, and asked where the rest of the 8-hour day went
- Caveat: the bar is illustrative, not a time study, and the unit count includes more than windshields. We can't call the other 5.5 hours wasted
- Travel, loading, parts checks, locating customers, and rescheduling all take real time. Some of it adds value, some is slack
- Ask (~30 sec): name one missing activity that creates value and one that signals avoidable slack
- Debrief: careful part checks look like lost time but prevent a failed trip, while idle waiting is pure waste. A good metric would reward the first and cut the second, but units per day can't tell them apart
- Handoff: "Economics gives us a framework for exactly this measurement problem."
- Source: HBS case p. 5 (time allocation illustrative)

**Slide 6: Output is easier to observe than effort** (3 min)
- Safelite is the principal, the technician is the agent. Safelite wants completed, safe, quality jobs, but pace, attention, and part checks are partly hidden. That's the moral hazard setup
- Under hourly pay, extra effort doesn't change this week's paycheck. Pride, promotion, and keeping the job still matter, but the immediate money reward is weak
- Output depends on effort, the operating system, demand, and luck (a concept, not an estimated equation)
- Ask (30 sec): if you can count installations, why is effort still hard to observe? Pause five seconds, take one answer
- Debrief: output is a noisy signal of effort. Identical effort can produce different output depending on the jobs you draw, and identical output can hide different effort. Paying on a noisy signal pushes risk onto the worker, so every bit of incentive Safelite adds has to be weighed against the risk it creates. That tradeoff drives the rest of the deck
- Optional follow-up: what extra information would make units per day a better signal? (route length, task mix, offered jobs, parts errors, rework)
- Handoff: "Now let's see exactly how the pay formula changes the reward."
- Source: economic interpretation, HBS case pp. 5, 8-9

**Slide 7: PPP rewards extra output above the guarantee** (2 min)
- Hourly line is flat: another unit doesn't change pay
- PPP line is flat at first, then rises. Piece earnings accumulate with each job, but take-home stays at the floor until they exceed it. In this drawing the floor equals former hourly earnings
- Ask (30 sec): when would one more installation add nothing to this week's paycheck? Let someone find the flat section before you explain it
- Debrief: the flat segment is the point. Below the crossover, extra effort earns nothing extra, so the incentive only bites once piece earnings pass the floor. With a $440 guarantee, $400→$420 changes nothing, and $430→$450 adds only $10. A floor protects pay but mutes motivation
- Cue: the dollar examples are illustrative, not reported outcomes. Don't say PPP pays the hourly guarantee plus all piece earnings
- Handoff: "That's the mechanism. Before we get into Safelite's exact plan, let's zoom out: what does any move to piece rate trade away?"
- Source: HBS case pp. 6-7 (thresholds illustrative)

**Slide 8: Piece rate carries the same tradeoffs in any job** (2 min)
- Piece rates give workers a direct financial reason to raise measured output, and can attract people who expect to earn more that way
- But output also depends on demand, equipment, and other people's work, and unmeasured things like quality and cooperation get less attention. Workers who value stable income need higher expected pay to take the risk
- Ask (20 sec): name a job where piece rate would fit poorly, and why. Take one example and one reason (team production, safety-critical work, variable conditions)
- Debrief: piece rate works when output is observable and individually attributable, which is exactly what a poor-fit job lacks. Safelite sits somewhere in the middle, which is why the details of its plan matter
- Handoff: "Now let's look at Safelite's actual contract, including the part that changes after 12 weeks."
- Source: general economic reasoning applied to the case

**Slide 9: A piece rate sits above a changing weekly guarantee** (2 min)
- First 12 weeks: Safelite pays the higher of piece earnings or earnings at the tech's previous hourly rate
- After 12 weeks: the guarantee rate drops about 30%, and the worker still gets whichever is higher
- Think of the guarantee as income insurance. Lowering it cuts protection when output is low, and lets piece earnings set actual pay at a lower output level
- Ask (20 sec): does a lower guarantee change the piece rate, or the point where it starts to matter?
- Debrief: it changes the threshold and the downside, not the rate per job. Less insurance plus piece pay taking over sooner makes the same plan both more motivating and riskier. The 12-week transition gives time to learn and adjust, but it doesn't solve a tech who starts in busy season and hits a slow winter
- Optional follow-up: what would you want a worker to experience in those 12 weeks before judging the plan?
- Handoff: "Safelite's worksheet makes the upside concrete, so let's test its assumptions."
- Source: HBS case p. 6

**Slide 10: A five-unit day could produce $608.96 in a sample week** (2 min)
- The math: 2 curved tempered parts at $19 plus 3 windshields at $20 = $98 a day, $490 a week. Other work adds $112.51 and sales add $6.45, for $608.96, or $15.22 an hour over 40 hours
- That beats the $10-12 experienced wage range, but it assumes five units a day, double the reported average
- Ask (30 sec): what must be true for $15.22 an hour to be realistic? Take two short conditions
- Debrief: $15.22 is an upside case, not an average. It needs enough feasible jobs, correct parts, and the right glass mix inside 40 hours, and rates varied by market. The real question is the distribution of weekly earnings including bad weeks, which the worksheet doesn't show
- Handoff: "To judge whether that upside is real, let's separate what workers control from what the company supplies."
- Source: HBS case p. 7, Table 1; wage comparison p. 6

**Slide 11: Who controls each source of lost output?** (4 min)
- Timing: 30 sec to introduce the columns, 2 min of discussion, 90 sec to summarize and transition
- Ask: do these columns assign responsibility correctly? Which factors should affect pay, and which call for an operational fix? Follow up: who can influence each factor, and how should pay respond?
- Cues: wrong parts are shared (the warehouse creates the error, the tech can sometimes catch it before leaving). A no-show customer is external, though confirmations can reduce it. Accept more than one allocation if the rule rewards useful prevention
- Debrief: pay should follow what the tech controls. The case reports wrong parts on 10 to 20% of jobs, so a big share of lost output isn't the tech's doing. Reward prevention, but don't charge the full cost of a warehouse or scheduling failure. Penalizing problem reports backfires too, because people start hiding them
- Handoff: "That leads straight to the question of who should bear the risk, starting with the guarantee."
- Source: HBS case pp. 5, 8-9

**Slide 12: Should PPP include a guaranteed minimum?** (2 min)
- For a floor: it makes a bad week survivable and may reduce discouragement while people learn or when Safelite supplies too few jobs. The company can also pool shocks better than one household. These are reasons to consider a floor, not measured morale effects
- Against: below the floor, another job adds nothing to take-home pay, so a threshold that feels unreachable gives little immediate reason to push. Removing the floor sharpens the link to output but can deter applicants or cause quits, and risk-averse workers want higher expected pay
- Ask (30 sec): keep, lower, or remove the minimum? Three-way vote, one reason
- Debrief: insurance and incentives pull in opposite directions. A higher floor protects workers but mutes the push to produce, and a lower floor sharpens the incentive but adds risk. Neither extreme wins automatically. What matters is whether techs have a realistic chance to beat the floor, which depends on reliable dispatch and coaching
- Handoff: "Seasonal demand makes this tradeoff concrete."
- Source: economic interpretation, HBS case pp. 6, 8-9 (motivation effects are hypotheses)

**Slide 13: Seasonal demand makes the guarantee matter** (2 min)
- Spring and summer were busy and employers competed for techs. Winter demand fell and layoffs were common. The bars are schematic; the case has no monthly volume series
- Workers also had outside options. Managers described people changing employers for a dollar more an hour or a take-home company truck, so labor supply responds to small changes in the package
- Ask (20 sec): should the guarantee change when winter brings too few jobs?
- Debrief: in winter, low output reflects demand, not effort, so a flat cut-down floor leaves workers exposed to something they can't control. A seasonal floor, or judging pay on jobs offered instead of completed output alone, fixes that, at a cost and only if clear rules and dispatch records back it up. And with competitors one dollar away, losing experienced workers is a real risk
- Optional follow-up: what dispatch record would separate low demand from low effort?
- Handoff: "Now let's put a dollar value on the protection Safelite proposed to remove."
- Source: HBS case p. 6

**Slide 14: The 30% guarantee cut is large in a household budget** (3 min)
- Focus on the middle row: $11 an hour × 40 hours = a $440 floor. A 30% cut makes the rate $7.70 and the weekly floor $308, which is $132 less guaranteed for the same hours
- It's a cut to the floor, not a prediction that every paycheck falls 30%. A tech with high enough piece earnings can still earn more, but the downside is now real. Rates varied by market, so the table doesn't give every tech the same opportunity
- Ask (30 sec): at $11 an hour today, would you accept PPP with a $308 weekly floor? Vote yes, no, or need more info, take one reason, and a contrasting one if time allows
- Debrief: higher potential earnings don't settle whether a worker says yes. A risk-averse worker can value a secure $440 over an uncertain upside, so the same cut reads as incentive to management and as a threat to the worker. Trust in dispatch decides which. Communication helps, but a clearer brochure won't fix an unreliable route or missing glass
- Optional follow-up: which single fact would most change your vote?
- Handoff: "Different answers to that question also predict who stays and who leaves."
- Source: HBS case pp. 6, 9; regional rates p. 7, Table 1 note a. Calculations assume 40 paid hours

**Slide 15: PPP changes who works at Safelite, not only how they work** (2 min)
- Two channels to separate. Effort effect: the same tech changes behavior under the new plan. Selection effect: the workforce changes because different people join, stay, or leave
- Fast techs expecting high piece earnings may find Safelite more attractive. A low floor can deter skilled applicants who need stable income. Some lower-output workers may leave, but the plan can also lose people for reasons unrelated to skill
- Ask (20 sec): could PPP drive away a highly skilled technician?
- Debrief: yes, and it matters. A skilled worker may value predictable income, have a weak route, or hold a strong outside offer, so a departure doesn't prove low productivity. This is screening through employment choice. Safelite doesn't need a menu of contracts for it to happen, but acceptance alone can't separate skill from risk tolerance. If average output rises, compare incumbents separately from entrants and leavers before crediting effort alone
- Optional follow-up: what information about departing workers would tell us whether turnover is costly?
- Handoff: "Even among the workers who stay, the pay formula can change which tasks get attention."
- Source: HBS case pp. 6, 8-9 (effort and selection channels are economic predictions, not reported post-rollout findings)
